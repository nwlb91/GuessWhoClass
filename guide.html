Find anyone in 5 questions or fewer

There are 24 faces. A yes/no question that splits the faces still standing in half means every answer leaves half as many: 24 → 12 → 6 → 3 → 2 → 1. That’s binary search. Because 24 isn’t a power of 2, the group of 3 can only split into 1 and 2, so some people are found in 4 questions and the rest in 5. Each question depends on the answers before it, so there’s no fixed list to memorize. Students follow the tree below.

The whole decision tree

Every question splits the faces still standing as evenly as possible, and the next question depends on the answer. People higher up the tree are found in 4 questions, the rest in 5.

Why it’s not obvious

Lots of features look like good first questions but don’t quite split the board in half. Only “Is it a man?” (or “Is it a woman?”) does. The same feature can also mean different things in different branches. A hat is the 2nd question for men, the 3rd question for women with earrings, and just a decoy for women without earrings.

With strategy hints turned on, the game shows each question’s split, like 12|12 or 10|14.

Lesson ideas

  1. Play first, explain later. Let students play a couple of rounds (or practice solo) and record how many questions they needed before they knew the answer.
  2. Find the best first question. Ask: “Which question flips down the most faces whatever the answer is?” Compare “Is it Grace?” (flips down 1 face or 23) with “Hat?” (10 or 14) and “Man?” (12 either way).
  3. Play with hints. Turn on Strategy hints in the lobby. The game shows how every question would split the faces still standing and stars the one from the tree.
  4. Notice the next question changes. After “Hat? yes” the tree asks about glasses, but after “Hat? no” it asks about a beard. Why does the best question depend on who’s left?
  5. Name the pattern. 24 → 12 → 6 → 3 → 2 → 1. Why can’t 4 questions always be enough for 24 people? (4 yes/no answers make only 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 different answer patterns.) How many questions for 32 faces? 64? 1,000? (5, 6, and 10, because 2¹⁰ = 1,024.)
  6. Connect it. Looking up a word in a dictionary, the “higher or lower” number game, and finding a page in a book all work the same way.

Running a game

  1. Click Host a game. You get a 4-character code and a QR code. Put it on the projector.
  2. The student scans the QR code (or opens this site and types the code), enters their name, and joins.
  3. You both pick a mystery person, and the student asks first. Questions from the list are answered automatically. Typed questions are answered by the other player.
  4. For the next student, open the menu (☰) and choose Invite a different student. To run several games at once, open the site in more browser tabs. Each tab hosts its own game with its own code.
  5. Students can also host games for each other. Anyone can click Host a game.

Games connect the two browsers directly (peer-to-peer). Nothing is stored on a server and no accounts are needed. If someone loses connection or refreshes, they reconnect to the same game automatically while the host’s page stays open.
